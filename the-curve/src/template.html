The Curve

Moore's Law holds that the power of a computer chip, measured by the transistors packed onto it, doubles about every two years. For sixty years it has proven stubbornly true. So what does AI's own growth look like against it - and how much of AI's exponent does the chip curve actually explain? Every line here is a real chip, model or accelerator, and every point has a source.

    Reading this page

    Transistors are per-chip counts for CPUs, systems-on-chip and GPUs from public specifications, as compiled on Wikipedia's Transistor count page. Multi-die packages count as one chip; wafer-scale parts are excluded. Training compute, parameters and accelerator throughput come from Epoch AI's open datasets, Notable AI Models and Machine Learning Hardware, both CC-BY 4.0. Throughput is dense FP16/BF16 tensor performance per accelerator, the figure that matters for training.

    The record line for each layer follows the highest value published up to that date. Doubling times are least-squares fits to the record line, in log space, over the period named; the fit needs a few points and at least a year. The comparison view divides each line by its own 2012 record so the four pass through one point and their slopes can be compared; 2012 is the year AlexNet made GPUs the engine of AI. The parameters layer leaves out a handful of pre-2010 systems whose published "parameter" counts are n-gram tables or kernel machines rather than learned weights. Milestones are context, not causes. This page re-fetches its sources and rebuilds itself on a schedule; the stamp at the top is the last run.
