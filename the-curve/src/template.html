The Curve

I've shown a version of this chart to rooms full of educators two years running, because most people have heard of Moore's Law but have never seen what sits on top of it. Chips have doubled their transistor count roughly every two years for fifty-five years, which is astonishing on its own. The AI curves stacked on that foundation double every five or six months. That gap is the thing I most wanted to see for myself, so this page starts there - every line is a real chip, model or test run, and every point has a source.

    Reading this page

    Transistors are per-chip counts for CPUs, systems-on-chip and GPUs from public specifications, as compiled on Wikipedia's Transistor count page. Multi-die packages count as one chip; wafer-scale parts are excluded. Training compute, parameters and accelerator throughput come from Epoch AI's open datasets: Notable AI Models and Machine Learning Hardware, both CC-BY 4.0. Throughput is dense FP16/BF16 tensor performance per accelerator, which is the figure that matters for training; it understates chips reported only at lower precisions.

    Benchmarks come from Epoch AI's Benchmarking Hub, which runs a fixed protocol per benchmark or collects external results under one; the AIME figures use OTIS mock exams, and the METR series is the length of software task, in human-minutes, that a model completes half the time. "Solved" here means the best score reached the threshold you pick, and the months are counted from the benchmark's publication date; models released before a benchmark were scored on it afterwards, which is why some lines start left of the gold tick.

    The record line for each layer follows the highest value published up to that date. Doubling times are least-squares fits to the record line, in log space, over whatever period is in view; the fit needs a few points and at least a year, so very narrow ranges show none. Indexing to 2012 is a choice, not a fact: it is the year AlexNet made GPUs the engine of AI, and it puts the four lines through one point so their slopes can be compared. The parameters layer leaves out a handful of pre-2010 systems whose published "parameter" counts are n-gram tables or kernel machines rather than learned weights, which would otherwise sit above every neural network until 2021.
